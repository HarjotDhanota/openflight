Radar orientation & camera placement

OpenFlight contributor reference — IWR6843LEVM radar, OV9281 camera, and Cormac’s enclosure design.

Reviewed 17 September 2026 · Proposed experiments, not an approved production configuration.

Keep the current enclosure and documented RX-vertical orientation as the baseline. First check camera fit, framing, and RF behavior with the camera above the antenna area. RX-center alignment is optional. Investigate radar rotation only if measurements identify a limitation that could justify the enclosure and processing changes. All directions below are viewed from the antenna side, looking at the board. “TI reference” means the orientation in TI’s board photograph, not a mandatory mounting direction. This reference applies to the LEVM, not the ISK, ODS, or antenna-on-package boards.

  1. Pick the orientation and verify the landmarks. Use A for RX horizontal or B for the documented 90° CCW arrangement. Measure from PCB edges, not the USB body.
  2. Choose a camera centerline that fits the enclosure. Optional alignment above the RX centroid is, in A: 27.573 mm from the left edge (27.427 from the right); in B: 14.739 mm from the left edge (53.718 from the right). These are convenient mechanical references, not an RF optimum or a required camera position. A lateral adjustment is reasonable if fit, framing, and RF checks pass; record and calibrate the resulting transform.
  3. Choose and record the remaining dimensions. Supply camera/lens geometry; set height h above the top edge and depth zC from the front PCB plane after checking the full assembly envelope. Include the lens-front-to-optical-center offset and camera pitch/yaw/roll.
  4. For the orientation comparison, hold the world geometry fixed. Ideally roll the radar about the RX centroid, keeping that point and the camera stationary. A fixture rotating about a different point must translate the board or record the changed transform. Do not move the camera merely to preserve its board-edge distance.

2. What rotating the radar changes

The TX1/TX3 × four-RX combination forms the longer, eight-element virtual array. TX2 adds an offset on the perpendicular axis. Rolling the board swaps which world direction receives the longer aperture. TI virtual-array geometry.

Expected consequences of the array geometry — not measured accuracy guarantees
ConfigurationPotential benefitTradeoff
RX verticalStronger vertical angular separation; useful for launch angle and separating direct returns from floor reflections.Less horizontal aperture for lateral separation and direction estimates.
RX horizontalStronger horizontal angular separation; potentially useful for horizontal launch and club-path tracking.Less vertical aperture; vertical tracking and floor-reflection rejection need renewed evaluation.

These are engineering expectations from the geometry. Angular resolution is not single-target angle accuracy. Better horizontal tracking does not directly measure clubface orientation or prove better impact-location estimates.

Coverage also rotates. TI specifies nominal 120° azimuth × 80° elevation FoV in the RX-horizontal reference orientation. With RX vertical, that becomes approximately 80° horizontal × 120° vertical about the radar boresight (±40° and ±60°, respectively). Apply the installed pitch/yaw when checking the enclosure. The wider vertical coverage matters for a lens protruding above the antenna region; FoV is neither angular resolution nor a sharp RF clearance boundary. TI user guide, section 1.1.

Software gate: current direction-of-arrival code uses a vertical array and a floor-image rejection rule. A horizontal installation needs reviewed axis/sign handling, channel geometry, multipath treatment, and new calibration before its angle or fused outputs are scored. Rotating the hardware and relabeling existing results is insufficient. Current processing code.

3. Camera above the radar: what we can actually conclude

Above the radar is not inherently worse for face angle or impact location. Optical evidence depends on the camera’s position relative to the ball and club, lens, sampling, exposure, and visible features—not the radar’s position underneath it. Rotating only the radar leaves the optical view unchanged.

Placement options to evaluate on Cormac’s design
OptionReason to try itWhat to check
Above, optionally centered on RXSimple mechanical reference; removes lateral offset to the RX centroid when aligned. Exact alignment is not required.Vertical/depth offsets remain. Check club/ball overlap, visible club features, framing, and radar interference.
Higher camera positionChanges the actual viewing elevation; may expose useful crown or topline features.May hide other features or change occlusion. No validated ideal height or accuracy advantage yet.
Lateral camera offsetChanges viewpoint and may separate overlapping features.Adds lateral parallax and potentially handedness-dependent occlusion; test both relevant swing directions.

Aim to retain the stationary ball, final club approach, contact region, and early ball flight. Tilting around the same optical center changes framing, not viewpoint. Do not inherit old enclosure heights, lens assumptions, or extrinsics.

Face angle requires an identifiable club orientation; an ambiguous rear silhouette may not provide it. Impact location additionally needs a registered clubface reference, depth and timing, plus independent strike-location truth. Neither endpoint has a demonstrated accuracy guarantee for the proposed layout.

RF clearance: keep the lens housing, camera PCB, fasteners, and cables out of the antenna’s forward region where practical. Above and behind the antenna plane is a starting layout, not proof of RF transparency. There is no validated universal camera-to-antenna clearance for this assembly; compare returns with the actual camera and enclosure installed. TI radome guidance.

4. Record the full geometry—not one average distance

The RX centroid is a valid coordinate origin. Keep the individual TX and RX positions in that coordinate system; choosing an origin does not discard their geometry. TX position matters because each measured signal travels from a particular transmitter to the target and back to a particular receiver.

  • Record the nominal 3D centers of all three TX and four RX groups, with channel labels, units, axis directions, and board revision. CAD group centers are starting references, not calibrated RF phase centers.
  • Record the camera-to-radar translation and rotation. Seven scalar camera-to-antenna distances are not a substitute for that transform.
  • Distinguish the measured lens-front reference from the camera optical center; solve camera intrinsics/distortion and radar–camera extrinsics at the installed focus and mode.
  • Survey the ball/target position, floor height, radar boresight, and camera view. Repeat geometry checks whenever a mount moves.

Per-channel amplitude and phase can help validate the array model against a surveyed reflector. They are not twelve independent ground-truth target positions. A camera pixel supplies a ray, not range; an optical club feature may also differ from the strongest radar scattering point.

5. Contributor test procedure

Agree on the comparison and acceptable endpoint errors with the maintainer before collecting a large dataset. Change one factor at a time. Keep failed and rejected captures.

First: decide camera placement within the current enclosure

Keep RX vertical and use the current camera mode for these checks. A full orientation comparison, camera-mode study, face-angle study, or timing study is not a prerequisite for this mechanical placement decision. Passing these checks establishes a candidate layout, not validated accuracy for every launch-monitor metric.

  1. Document the assembly. Photograph the antenna side and full rig. Record Cormac’s design revision, board/camera/lens identification, mounting measurements, software commit, radar profile, and lighting.
  2. Check fit and framing. Fit the actual camera, lens, bracket, fasteners, and cables above the radar. Check their envelope against the antenna coverage at the installed tilt. Confirm the stationary ball, final club approach, contact region, and early ball flight remain in frame. Adjust the camera centerline if needed and record its pose.
  3. Establish the supported baseline. With RX vertical, capture surveyed stationary targets at several ranges and horizontal/vertical positions. Register a visible marker to the radar reflector with a measured offset. Reserve some positions for validation rather than calibration.
  4. Check camera/enclosure interference. At unchanged radar pose and settings, compare camera absent, installed but unpowered, and powered/capturing. Check the final shell too. Retain raw channel data; compare detection availability, noise, amplitude, relative phase, and target estimates.

Placement decision: retain the current orientation and enclosure if fit, framing, and RF behavior meet the agreed requirements. If they do not, first investigate the measured obstruction, interference, or visibility problem. Consider radar rotation only when evidence points to an orientation-dependent limitation; require a repeatable benefit on a temporary fixture before committing to an enclosure redesign.

Follow-up studies: address a measured limitation

Select the study relevant to the limitation. Hold the other variables fixed; these studies can proceed separately from the initial placement decision.

  1. Compare radar orientations. Hold camera pose, mode, exposure, focus, lighting, and target geometry fixed. Record any unavoidable radar-center movement. Alternate RX-vertical / RX-horizontal / RX-vertical to expose drift. Horizontal raw captures are useful before software support; label derived outputs unvalidated until the software gate in section 2 is met.
  2. Compare camera positions separately. Fix the radar orientation. Recalibrate each camera position and compare visibility, ball/club pixel span, localization uncertainty, clipping, blur, and valid pre-impact observations across drivers, irons, and wedges.
  3. Evaluate actual endpoints. Compare against independent reference measurements where available. Report signed bias, absolute error, upper-tail error, sample count, and failure rate—not only attractive example frames. Separate calibration captures from held-out evaluation swings.

Camera-mode study: include binned modes

After the placement comparison, test the available native and binned/subsampled OV9281 modes separately. Start with matched delivered cadence, then vary cadence and exposure. Record the resolved sensor readout, crop, binning/skipping configuration, bit depth, exposure, gain, and achieved frame intervals. Output dimensions alone do not identify the readout. Measure sensitivity and noise rather than assuming all reduced-resolution modes are pure skipping or that they provide a fixed light gain. Sensor documentation.

Timing: measure it before adding hardware

Preserve sensor timestamps, exposure information, radar chirp/frame timing, and trigger/clock metadata. Compare independently identified events; do not “validate” synchronization using a camera estimate already anchored to radar contact. Report offset, jitter, and clock drift. This protocol requires neither an LED nor a new timing board. Any electrical synchronization proposal follows a measured timing shortfall, with a separate hardware review.

6. What to send back

Use one study archive, grouped by radar orientation → camera position → camera mode → run. Give corresponding camera, radar, and reference records the same run/shot identifier.

  • Manifest: hardware and software versions, geometry and units, settings, timestamps/clock definitions, lighting, club, test condition, and missing or failed captures.
  • Original evidence: camera frames/video with metadata, available raw radar captures and profiles, calibration inputs/results, assembly photos, and independent reference data.
  • Short report: question tested, controlled variables, sample counts, measured differences, uncertainty, failure cases, and proposed next step. Mark unmeasured quantities as unknown.

This page is a standalone reference, not a capture controller. The separate camera-study runner packages its camera-study artifacts; it does not automatically provide this full orientation experiment, collect every radar stream, or implement rotated-array processing. Verify archive contents before sharing.

Share raw data through the agreed storage channel, not Git. Send a small report and archive link first. Any proposed code, geometry, or default-setting change must remain a focused, maintainer-reviewed change; data collection is not approval to deploy it.

7. Evidence and limits

Do not carry forward superseded claims: August rig dimensions were assumptions, and the earlier sub-frame timing validation was circular. Old optics calculations do not establish an optimal camera height, a universal face-angle penalty, or a fixed “15× more light” requirement.
